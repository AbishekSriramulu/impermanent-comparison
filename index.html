Impermanent benchmark

Are foundation models good for intermittent data?

Forecasts from pretrained models, locally fitted models and SeasonalNaive on 1,600 GitHub event series and 29 historical origins. Every model has access to the same expanding history at each issue.

Metric definitions and aggregation

Lower scores are better. A case is one series at one forecast origin. A lead is one forecast step. Each training scale uses only observations available for that series at that origin.

Both CRPS scores use the same quantile loss. For observed count y and predicted quantile q at level τ, pinball loss is τ × max(y − q, 0) + (1 − τ) × max(q − y, 0). The raw case score is twice the average of this loss over the forecast leads and the nine levels 0.1 through 0.9.

Scaled CRPS9
Divide each raw quantile case score by max(mean of observed training counts, 1). Apply the arithmetic average described below. The scale measures the series’ historical activity, and every eligible case contributes to the average.

What it emphasizes. The same count error costs more on a quieter series, up to the scale floor. A large scaled miss can outweigh several small improvements. Calibration by quantile or dependence across forecast leads requires additional diagnostics.

Paper CRPS
Take the median of the raw quantile case scores within each category and origin, then divide by that cell’s Zero reference. Take the median across all included category–origin cells. Each cell contributes one value to the final median, regardless of its series count. Frequencies with more cells contribute more values.

What it emphasizes. The typical Zero-relative cell. Severe misses on a minority of series or origins can leave the medians unchanged. Many zero-loss cells can produce a final score of zero alongside positive losses elsewhere.

RMSSE
For each case, divide the mean squared point error over leads by the mean squared difference between consecutive training observations. Take the square root for each case, then apply the arithmetic average. The common eligibility mask excludes cases with a zero squared-difference scale.

What it emphasizes. Large misses within multi-step forecasts and errors on series that usually change little. At one lead the formula is an absolute error divided by the training root mean square difference. A partly flat history can give an eligible case a small denominator.

MASE
For each case, divide the mean absolute point error over leads by max(mean absolute difference between consecutive training observations, 0.01). Apply the arithmetic average.

What it emphasizes. The size of a point error relative to routine changes. At a fixed scale losses rise linearly with absolute error. Small training scales give quiet series more weight per unit of error. Use the probability scores to compare forecast uncertainty.

Public MASE
Take the median of the case MASE scores within each category and origin, then divide by that cell’s Zero reference. Average the normalized cells with the same recorded cutoff, then average those cutoff scores. Cells that share a cutoff split its weight, including across frequencies. A frequency with more distinct cutoffs can have more influence overall.

What it emphasizes. The size of changes in typical category errors. The first median can hide large minority misses. A small Zero denominator can amplify a cell’s contribution to the cutoff average.

Arithmetic average. For Scaled CRPS9, RMSSE and MASE, average eligible series equally at each origin, average origins equally within each frequency, then average the included frequencies equally. In the all-frequency view here, monthly gets the same one-third weight as daily. One of the two monthly origins therefore has seven times the total weight of one of the 14 daily origins.

Why the rankings can differ. Suppose A scores 1 and 1 on two daily origins and 6 on one weekly origin. B scores 3 on all three. With one category per origin and three distinct cutoffs, A wins two origins and has the lower median (1 vs 3) and cutoff average (2.67 vs 3). B wins on the equal-frequency average (3 vs 3.50), since weekly gets half the weight. Large weighted gains can offset many small losses. Use the paired win counts to see how often a model beats SeasonalNaive, and the frequency and sparsity filters to see where.

Zero reference. A category combines an event subdataset and a sparsity band. For each category, frequency and origin, score a forecast of zero using the same case loss. The denominator is the greater of the median Zero loss and the 10th percentile of strictly positive Zero losses, using linear interpolation. If all Zero losses are zero, use 0.001. This applies to Paper CRPS and Public MASE.

Calibration and interval width

Coverage is the share of observations inside the q10–q90 interval, including its endpoints. Scaled width is (q90 − q10) divided by the training activity scale used in Scaled CRPS9. The two median rates count observations below q50 and at or below q50. These diagnostics use the same arithmetic averaging across leads, series, origins and frequencies.

Evaluation methods

The historical panel contains 14 daily, 13 weekly and two monthly origins with 7, 1 and 1 leads respectively. Sparsity groups follow the frozen Impermanent labels.

The shared history policy was selected by a Development-only comparison with a two-year window. Models use timestamp-derived calendar inputs through their supported interfaces. Tree and statistical controls use lead-specific residual quantiles from earlier, non-overlapping Development origins. The forecast archives preserve each model’s point and distribution outputs.
